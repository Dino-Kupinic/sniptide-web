In March 2002, the people in the #php channel on IRC had a small problem. Someone would ask why their script failed, paste forty lines of code into the channel, and bury every other conversation under it. Paul Dixon, one of the regulars, wrote a page that took the code and gave back a link. It went live on 3 September 2002 at pastebin.com.

Dixon's own README for the original code says it plainly: "This is the source code to the original pastebin.com, which I started back in 2002." It was one PHP script that grew as people asked for things. Twenty-four years later, the word pastebin names a whole category of website, and the site itself has hosted everything from homework to the passwords of a million people.

## A link instead of forty lines

A pastebin solves a chat problem, not a code problem. A link takes one line in the channel, and only the people who want to help have to read the code. Everyone else keeps talking.

That trade sounds small, and for years it was. Pastebin took eight years to reach its first million active pastes, according to TechCrunch, where "active" means pastes that haven't expired and aren't spam.

## Sold because of the copyright notices

By 2010, running the site had stopped being a hobby. Dixon later wrote that he "sold the domain in 2010 due to the time required to deal with all the copyright issues", and told TechCrunch it had become "too much of a time drain".

The buyer was Jeroen Vader, a Dutch entrepreneur who saw the site listed for sale in February 2010. "Pastebin is actually the only website I have ever owned that I didn't create myself," he told The Next Web the following year. When he bought it, he said, the design and interface hadn't changed in about eight years.

## The year Pastebin became a notice board

Then the internet found a new use for it. A paste needs no account, shows up in the public list of recent pastes, and stays up until someone removes it. For a group that had just broken into a server, that made it the perfect place to publish what they took.

The Next Web counted the big ones in June 2011:

- In December 2010, a group called Gnosis posted 1.3 million usernames and passwords from Gawker Media.
- In May 2011, LulzSec posted more than 70,000 records from a Fox talent show database.
- On 2 June 2011, LulzSec posted what it said was data on more than a million Sony users. Google's cache showed 155,000 views of that one paste.

About half of the top 60 trending pastes at the time were related to hacked servers. The site also stored about 20,000 new pastes on an ordinary day, most of them still code, but the leaks were what made the news.

The numbers grew with the attention. Ten million active pastes arrived in October 2011, a year and a half after the first million.

## Hiring people to read the pastes

By April 2012 the site drew 17 million unique visitors a month, up from 500,000 two years earlier, and received about 1,200 abuse reports a day. Vader told the BBC that the report button "isn't responsive enough" and that he planned to hire staff to look for dumps of personal data and remove them, The Register reported. The site had already been blocked in Pakistan and Turkey over pastes, and Vader said that in three months not a single day had passed without a denial-of-service attack.

Members of Anonymous called it censorship. On 19 April 2012, Anonymous and a group calling itself the People's Liberation Front announced AnonPaste, a pastebin whose servers cannot read what they host, and so cannot be asked to remove it.

## The encrypted answer

AnonPaste was "based on the open source ZeroBin software", which the French developer Sébastien Sauvage had released a week earlier, on 12 April 2012. ZeroBin encrypts each paste with 256-bit AES in the browser and puts the key in the part of the link after the `#`. Browsers never send that part to the server, so, in Sauvage's words, the server "cannot decrypt data".

ZeroBin's repository now points to PrivateBin, its maintained successor. Pastebin's moderation problem produced a lasting idea: if the host never sees the text, there is nothing for the host to read, sell or delete.

Four years earlier, GitHub had taken the opposite direction. Gist, demonstrated in July 2008, stored each paste in its own Git repository, so a snippet could be cloned, edited and given a history. Between Gist and ZeroBin, the two ideas later pastebins keep returning to were in place by 2012: a paste as a versioned file, and a paste the server cannot read.

## Most pastes were never public

The leaks shaped Pastebin's reputation, but they were never most of what it held. In June 2015 the site counted 65 million active pastes, and the owners said about 75 percent were unlisted or private. By December 2015 it held 95 million active pastes and more than two million members.

Most people, in other words, were using it the way Dixon's channel did. They needed a link to give one person, and the public list was a side effect.

## Pastebin closes the front window

In April 2020, Pastebin removed its built-in search and restricted its scraping API, which people had used to collect every new public paste as it appeared. Pastes from logged-out users stopped appearing in the recent list. Later that year it added password-protected pastes and pastes that delete themselves after one view.

Each of those changes moved Pastebin back toward its first job: a link for the people you choose, and less of a public board for everyone else.

## What 24 years of pastes add up to

Pastebin was built to save a chat channel forty lines, and almost every turn in its story comes from one fact about a paste: whoever has the link can read it. That made it useful in 2002, it made it a leak site in 2011, and it pushed the next generation of tools toward encryption, passwords and accounts.
